# How to calculate business runway

Business runway is the amount of time your company can keep operating before it runs out of cash, assuming current cash reserves and spending patterns stay the same. It is one of the most important metrics for founders, finance teams, and operators because it answers a very practical question: how long can we keep going without raising more money or changing our spending?

Runway is not the same as profitability. A business can be growing and still have a short runway if cash is being burned quickly. Similarly, a business can be profitable but still face a short runway if there are big seasonal swings or delayed customer payments.

## Why runway matters

Understanding runway helps you make decisions before a cash problem becomes urgent. It tells you:

- how many months you can operate with current cash
- whether you need to raise capital soon
- whether you should cut costs or slow hiring
- how much cash cushion you need for uncertainty
- when your cash position is becoming risky

For early-stage businesses, runway can be a survival metric. For more established businesses, it is a planning tool that helps shape spending, hiring, and investment decisions.

## The basic runway formula

The most common calculation is:

Runway = cash balance / monthly burn

Where:

- cash balance is the amount of cash available on hand
- monthly burn is the average net cash outflow per month

If a business has $600,000 in cash and is burning $50,000 per month, runway is:

$600,000 / $50,000 = 12 months

That means the company has roughly 12 months of operating runway before cash runs out, assuming spending remains stable.

## A more realistic version

In practice, the simple formula is useful, but it often needs adjustment. Many businesses do not spend evenly across the month, and their cash flow can fluctuate due to:

- seasonality
- delayed customer payments
- one-off costs
- hiring and payroll timing
- tax obligations
- equipment purchases

A more realistic runway model uses a cash-flow forecast rather than a single monthly average. You can still start with the simple formula, but you should update it as your business becomes more predictable.

## Step 1: calculate your burn rate

The first step is understanding your average monthly burn.

Burn is usually measured as:

Monthly burn = average monthly operating cash outflow - average monthly operating cash inflow

If your business spends $120,000 a month in total operating costs and brings in $70,000 a month in revenue, your net burn is:

$120,000 - $70,000 = $50,000 per month

If your business is in a pre-revenue stage, you may be measuring burn as all cash outflows minus any offsetting incoming cash.

## Step 2: measure your available cash

Use the cash balance that is truly available, not just the amount sitting in a bank account at a moment in time.

For runway, the most relevant figure is usually:

- cash on hand
- less any restricted cash
- less any committed near-term obligations that you cannot easily move

If a large amount of cash is reserved for taxes, payroll, or debt service, it may not be available for general spending. A conservative runway estimate is often more useful than an optimistic one.

## Step 3: divide cash by burn

Once you know the metrics, the calculation is straightforward:

Runway in months = cash balance / (monthly burn)

Example:

- cash on hand: $1,200,000
- monthly burn: $100,000
- runway: 12 months

This gives a simple estimate, but it should not be treated as a guaranteed timeline. It assumes the business keeps burning at the same rate and does not have unusual cash requirements.

## Step 4: adjust for uncertainty

A runway estimate is only as strong as the assumptions behind it. Most business owners should build in a buffer.

A conservative approach includes:

- slower sales than forecast
- delayed customer payments
- slower hiring or lower cost savings than expected
- unexpected legal, tax, or operational costs
- cash needed for inventory or equipment

For example, if your runway estimate is 12 months, you might plan for a more conservative buffer of 6 to 9 months. That gives you room to act before the cash position becomes critical.

## Step 5: use a runway forecast for better decisions

Monthly average burn can be misleading if cash flow changes over time. A runway forecast model adds more context by projecting cash balance month by month.

A typical forecast includes:

- opening cash balance
- expected revenue
- expected expenses
- payroll and taxes
- one-off investments or debt repayments
- cash before financing
- ending cash balance

This helps you answer questions like:

- when will cash fall below a safe threshold?
- what happens if revenue is delayed by 30 days?
- do we need to raise more capital before the next milestone?
- can we hire another person without pushing runway too low?

## Example runway calculation

Imagine a business has:

- cash on hand: $900,000
- average monthly revenue: $180,000
- average monthly operating costs: $260,000
- monthly net burn: $80,000

Runway = $900,000 / $80,000 = 11.25 months

That means the company has around 11 months of runway. If revenue slips or costs rise, the runway shortens quickly. If the company wants a stronger margin of safety, it may need to either cut spending or increase revenue before the cash cushion gets too thin.

## Quick rules of thumb

A few simple benchmarks can help frame runway decisions:

- under 6 months: high risk
- 6 to 12 months: manageable, but requires close monitoring
- 12 to 18 months: healthier, though still needs planning
- 18+ months: more flexibility, but not a reason to ignore cash discipline

These numbers vary by industry and business model, but they are useful signals for leadership teams.

## When runway is too short

A short runway usually means one of two things:

- the business is burning more cash than expected
- the business has not yet reached a sustainable revenue level

Either way, the response should be proactive. Common actions include:

- reducing discretionary spend
- delaying non-essential hires
- tightening payment terms with customers
- improving collections and cash conversion cycles
- raising capital sooner rather than later

In many cases, companies wait too long to act because they assume runway will improve naturally. The better approach is to monitor runway regularly and respond early.

## Best practices for managing runway

To keep runway under control, use these habits:

- track runway monthly, or even weekly for cash-sensitive businesses
- separate recurring and one-off costs
- model several scenarios: base case, conservative case, and downside case
- build a cash buffer for uncertainty
- look beyond gross revenue and focus on actual cash conversion

The goal is not only to know your runway, but to maintain enough flexibility to make thoughtful decisions when the market changes.

## Final thought

Business runway is a simple metric with a powerful purpose: it tells you how much time you have before cash pressure forces hard decisions. The basic formula is easy to calculate, but the real value comes from using it wisely.

A good runway model is realistic, conservative, and tied to actual business operations. When you measure it consistently, you are better equipped to plan hiring, spending, and fundraising before the next cash squeeze arrives.

If you want your business to stay resilient, runway should be part of your regular operating rhythm, not just a metric you check when a problem appears.
