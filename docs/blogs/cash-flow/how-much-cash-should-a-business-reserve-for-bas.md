# How much cash should a business reserve for BAS?

One of the biggest cash-flow surprises for small businesses is the BAS bill. Even a healthy business can feel stretched when a quarter-end tax payment lands and the cash is tied up in unpaid invoices, stock, or payroll.

The right answer is not a single fixed number for every business. A better rule is to reserve enough cash to cover the expected BAS liability, plus a buffer for timing and uncertainty.

## Why BAS creates a cash problem

A BAS is usually a quarterly payment that includes:

- GST you have collected from customers
- GST you have paid on business purchases
- PAYG withholding from wages and contractor payments
- sometimes PAYG instalments, depending on the business setup

That means the amount is often large, and it can arrive before you have received all the cash from customers or sales that were meant to fund it.

This is why businesses can be profitable on paper and still be short on cash when the BAS is due.

## A practical rule of thumb

A simple starting point is:

- reserve the expected BAS amount
- add a 10% to 20% buffer
- keep that money separate from operating cash

If your business has a stable pattern, a reserve of about 1.1x to 1.2x your expected BAS liability is a sensible starting point.

For example:

- expected GST and PAYG liability: $25,000
- 15% buffer: $3,750
- recommended reserve: about $28,750

This helps cover timing differences and protects you from a slightly higher-than-expected bill.

## A more accurate formula

A more practical way to work it out is:

Reserve = (GST collected - GST paid) + PAYG withheld + PAYG instalments due + buffer

Then add a buffer based on how predictable your cash flow is.

A common approach is:

- stable businesses: 10% buffer
- seasonal businesses: 20% to 30% buffer
- volatile or invoice-heavy businesses: 30%+ buffer

The more uncertain the timing of customer payments, the more cash you should set aside.

## Example

Imagine a business expects this BAS:

- GST collected: $18,000
- GST paid on expenses: $7,000
- PAYG withholding: $12,000
- total BAS due: $23,000

A 15% buffer would bring the reserve to:

- $23,000 x 1.15 = $26,450

That means the business should ideally keep at least $26,450 in a dedicated reserve account before the BAS due date.

If the business has a seasonal slowdown, or customers often pay late, then a larger cushion may be wise.

## How much should a business keep on hand?

There is no universal perfect number, but most businesses should aim to have enough cash to cover at least:

- the next BAS bill
- the next payroll cycle
- a small working capital buffer

For many small businesses, the key question is not “What is the minimum?” but “What is the safe amount before the tax bill falls due?”

If your BAS is usually around $20,000 to $40,000, many businesses should aim to keep close to that amount available in cash, plus a buffer.

## A conservative planning approach

If your business has uneven revenue, slower payment terms, or big seasonal swings, it is better to plan conservatively.

A helpful rule is:

- hold the expected BAS liability in a separate reserve account
- keep a second buffer for the next payroll or operating period
- avoid treating all of your available cash as spendable cash

This reduces the risk of having a large tax bill arriving when the bank account has already been committed elsewhere.

## Why a separate BAS reserve helps

A dedicated reserve account makes the process much easier.

It helps you:

- avoid spending the money you already know is due to the ATO
- reduce stress around quarter-end liabilities
- see your real available cash more clearly
- improve decision-making for hiring, stock purchases, or investing

When tax money is sitting in the same account as everyday operating funds, it is easy to forget that the money is not truly available.

## What to do if cash is tight

If the business is struggling to hold a full BAS reserve, the first step is to understand the timing gap.

Ask:

- Are customers paying late?
- Are expenses due before revenue is received?
- Is the business carrying too much stock or inventory?
- Are there seasonal dips that need a stronger buffer?

Sometimes the issue is not the BAS size itself, but the mismatch between when cash comes in and when the tax is due.

In those cases, improving cash collection and tightening the cash forecast can do more than simply trying to “push through” with a thinner reserve.

## A healthy baseline

A good baseline for many small businesses is:

- expected BAS due
- plus 10% to 20% buffer
- plus enough cash for payroll and short-term operating obligations

If your cash flow is stable, this is often enough to prevent tax surprises. If your business is seasonal or lumpy, a bigger reserve is usually more appropriate.

## Final thought

There is no single magic number for BAS reserve, but the right question is simple: “How much cash do I need to cover this tax bill without disrupting operations?”

A practical answer is to reserve your expected BAS liability, then add a buffer of 10% to 30% depending on how predictable the business is. Keep it separate, review it regularly, and update it as your cash cycle changes.

The businesses that stay calm around BAS time are usually the ones that treat tax as a planned cash obligation, not an unexpected surprise.
