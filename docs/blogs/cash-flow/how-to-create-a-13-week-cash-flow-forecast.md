# How to create a 13-week cash-flow forecast

A 13-week cash-flow forecast is one of the simplest and most useful financial planning tools for any small business. It helps you answer a practical question: “Do I have enough cash to cover payroll, supplier bills, taxes, and operating costs over the next three months?”

Unlike an annual budget, a 13-week cash-flow forecast focuses on short-term timing. That makes it much more useful when you need to act quickly. If sales are slower than expected, or a customer pays late, the forecast shows the risk early enough to adjust before it becomes a crisis.

## Why it matters

Most businesses do not fail because they are unprofitable in the long term. They fail because cash runs out at the wrong time. A 13-week forecast helps you:

- spot low-cash periods before they happen
- plan for payroll, tax, and supplier payments
- decide whether you can invest in growth
- identify when you may need a line of credit or invoice financing
- reduce surprises for yourself and your team

When you review it weekly, it becomes a working management tool rather than a static spreadsheet.

## Start with the right structure

Set up a spreadsheet or planning table with one row per week and columns for:

- week start date
- expected incoming cash
- expected outgoing cash
- net cash movement
- opening balance
- closing balance

A simple weekly format is enough to get started. You can add more detail later, such as customer names, invoice due dates, payroll dates, or tax obligations.

## Step 1: list all expected cash inflows

The first half of the forecast is your cash coming in. This includes:

- customer payments
- invoice collections
- loan receipts
- interest income
- any other cash-based income

Be realistic about timing. If a customer usually pays 14 days after sending an invoice, do not assume the money lands the same week. Use your actual payment history to estimate likely collection timing.

A good rule is to separate inflows into:

- confirmed cash: already invoiced and likely to be paid on time
- probable cash: likely, but less certain
- possible cash: early-stage or uncertain receipts

This helps you avoid overestimating cash and building a false sense of security.

## Step 2: list all cash outflows

The second half of the forecast covers all the money leaving your business. This usually includes:

- wages and contractor payments
- rent and utilities
- software subscriptions
- supplier bills
- marketing spend
- tax payments
- loan repayments
- equipment or inventory purchases

It is important to include fixed costs and variable costs separately. Fixed costs are easier to predict. Variable costs may change with volume, so estimate them based on recent trends rather than wishful thinking.

## Step 3: calculate the weekly net cash position

Once you have your expected inflows and outflows for each week, calculate:

Net cash movement = inflows - outflows

Then work out your closing cash balance:

Closing balance = opening balance + net cash movement

This creates a simple view of whether your business is building or draining cash each week.

## Step 4: include a buffer for uncertainty

A forecast is only useful if it reflects reality. It is common for a business to underestimate delays, seasonal dips, or one-off costs. Add a buffer to protect the forecast from optimism.

For example:

- assume slower collections from less reliable customers
- include a small contingency for unexpected expenses
- apply a conservative estimate to revenue that has not been received yet

This does not mean you are planning for failure. It means you are building a realistic view of cash risk.

## Step 5: track the critical points

When you review the 13-week forecast, focus on the weeks where the closing balance gets low or negative. These are your critical periods.

Ask questions like:

- Are there any weeks where cash falls below a safe minimum?
- Do payroll or tax due dates coincide with low-cash weeks?
- Can customer payment timing be improved?
- Are there late-paying clients driving the risk?
- Do I need to delay spending or push a payment date?

This is where the forecast starts to drive action.

## Step 6: review weekly and update it often

A 13-week forecast should be living, not static. Update it every week using actual results:

- actual invoices paid
- actual expenses incurred
- changes in customer payment behavior
- supplier payment changes
- new contracts or delayed deals

The more accurate the inputs, the more reliable the forecast becomes. Over time, you will start spotting patterns that affect your cash rhythm.

## A simple example

Here is a basic 13-week view:

| Week | Opening balance | Inflows | Outflows | Net cash | Closing balance |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | $40,000 | $12,000 | $18,000 | -$6,000 | $34,000 |
| 2 | $34,000 | $10,000 | $16,500 | -$6,500 | $27,500 |
| 3 | $27,500 | $15,000 | $14,000 | $1,000 | $28,500 |
| 4 | $28,500 | $9,000 | $20,000 | -$11,000 | $17,500 |
| 5 | $17,500 | $18,000 | $17,000 | $1,000 | $18,500 |
| 6 | $18,500 | $11,000 | $19,500 | -$8,500 | $10,000 |

This makes the risk obvious: even though revenue is healthy over time, the business has several low-cash weeks. That raises a clear management question: do you need to speed up collections, reduce discretionary spend, or arrange funding before week 4 or 6?

## Best practices for a useful forecast

To keep your forecast practical, use these habits:

- start with cash, not profit
- base estimates on recent actuals where possible
- keep the forecast to 13 weeks so it stays actionable
- review it weekly, not monthly
- highlight the minimum cash balance and the risk weeks
- separate committed payments from optional spend

## When to act on the forecast

A forecast only helps if you use it. If your closing balance is falling into a risky range, act early. Options may include:

- chasing overdue invoices
- renegotiating payment terms
- reducing discretionary spend
- delaying non-essential hiring or purchases
- arranging a short-term funding facility

The point is not to predict the future perfectly. It is to understand the next 13 weeks well enough to make better decisions before cash pressure becomes urgent.

## Final thought

A 13-week cash-flow forecast is a discipline, not a one-off spreadsheet. It helps you see the timing of cash movement, not just the overall financial picture. When you keep it updated and use it as a decision-making tool, it becomes one of the clearest ways to avoid cash surprises and keep your business stable.

If you review your forecast every week and treat it as a practical operating rhythm, you will be far more prepared for both the slow periods and the opportunity periods ahead.
